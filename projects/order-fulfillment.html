System scope

Order fulfillment spans operations that can succeed and fail independently. Payment may complete before inventory fails. Inventory may be reserved before shipping fails. A worker can restart midway through the process.

Workflow history lives in Temporal so the business process can outlive the worker executing it.

How the system works

FastAPI accepts authenticated order submissions and starts a Temporal workflow. Temporal persists workflow history independently of the worker process while activities execute the individual business steps.

Failure classification

Failures are separated into conditions that may improve with time and conditions that require a business decision or new input before retry policy is applied.

RetryableTransient infrastructure failureBounded retries with exponential backoff can succeed after a temporary dependency problem clears.
RetryableTemporary service unavailabilityAnother attempt can be useful because the underlying condition may change.
Non-retryableInvalid payment detailsRepeating the same operation does not correct invalid business input.
Non-retryableUnavailable inventory or invalid addressThese conditions require a business response rather than more infrastructure retries.
Retry rule Retry only when another attempt can plausibly change the outcome.

This prevents infrastructure retry machinery from turning permanent business failures into noisy, slow, repeated work.

Partial-success recovery

The workflow uses Saga-style compensating actions rather than treating external side effects like an atomic database transaction. When a later step fails, completed actions are reversed in the opposite order where possible.

01Payment succeeds

The order now has an external side effect that may need compensation later.

02Inventory reserved

A second completed side effect is added to the workflow state.

03Shipping fails

The workflow stops forward progress because a later business step cannot complete.

04Inventory released

The most recent completed side effect is compensated first.

05Payment refunded

Compensation continues in reverse order until prior completed work is addressed.

Recovery model Compensation is business logic, not rollback magic.

External actions can fail independently, so the workflow makes compensation order explicit and testable instead of pretending the entire process is one atomic transaction.

Cancellation

The workflow checks for cancellation at defined points so completed work can be compensated when necessary and later activities can be prevented from starting.

The test suite verifies cancellation after payment and after inventory reservation, including the expected compensation path for each state.

Worker-failure exercise

A controlled worker restart tests durability directly. Temporal retains workflow history while the worker is unavailable. When the worker returns, execution continues from persisted workflow state instead of reconstructing the business process from memory.

Measured result

One local Docker Compose run completed 25 of 25 authenticated order submissions successfully, with an average submission latency of 38.74 ms and p95 submission latency of 75.51 ms.

These measurements cover sequential order submission in the local environment. They are not production-capacity claims and do not measure the full duration of payment, inventory, shipping, and notification work.

Lessons from the workflow

Temporal provides durable workflow history and repeatable orchestration, but real external side effects would still require strong idempotency guarantees. A retry against a payment or shipping provider must not duplicate the side effect simply because the workflow itself is durable.

The project emits structured operational events, but it does not yet include distributed tracing, production alerting, or dependency-specific SLOs.

Limits of the lab

Payment, inventory, shipping, and notification integrations are simulated intentionally so the project can isolate workflow durability and failure handling without third-party credentials or sandboxes.

A production implementation would still need real external integrations, end-to-end idempotency controls, managed identity and secrets, distributed tracing, production alerting, larger concurrent load and recovery tests, and SLOs derived from measured dependency behavior.
